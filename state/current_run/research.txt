**1. CORE ANSWER**  
The “Great Library of Alexandria” was not erased in a single, dramatic blaze by the Arabs in 642 AD.  Scholarly evidence shows two distinct, historically documented destructions that ended the two Alexandrian libraries that ever existed:  

* **48 BC – Julius Caesar’s siege:** A fire set to destroy the Egyptian fleet spread to the royal Mouseion and burned a large portion of its scrolls. The loss was largely collateral; some works survived because they had been copied elsewhere.  

* **391 AD – Theophilus’s anti‑pagan purge:** Emperor Theodosius I’s decree to demolish pagan temples led Bishop Theophilus of Alexandria to raze the Serapeum (the later public library). Its book‑rooms were smashed and burned, wiping out most of the remaining unique collection.  

By the time the Arab army entered Alexandria in 642 AD there was no organized “Alexandrian library” left to burn.  The story of Amr ibn Al‑‘Āṣ using the books as fuel first appears in 12th‑century Muslim chronicles and is now regarded as a later invention.  What survived the two confirmed destructions are the works that had already been duplicated in other Mediterranean centres and the handful of scrolls that later entered the Byzantine and Islamic manuscript traditions.  

**2. STORY BEATS**  

1. **Founding (c. 295 BC).** Ptolemy I creates the royal Mouseion, the “Great Library.”  
2. **Peak (3rd c. BC).** The library amasses scrolls, sponsors scholars, and runs a scriptorium.  
3. **First fire (48 BC).** Caesar’s fleet‑burning fire spreads to the Mouseion; many scrolls are lost.  
4. **Partial rescue.** Some scrolls survive in the Serapeum, private collections, or duplicate copies.  
5. **Serapeum established (mid‑3rd c. BC).** A “daughter” public library is built in the temple of Serapis on Pharos.  
6. **Gradual attrition (3rd‑4th c. AD).** Minor fires, earthquakes, and empire‑wide decay chip away at the collection.  
7. **Imperial edict (391 AD).** Theodosius I orders the destruction of pagan temples.  
8. **Theophilus’s attack (391 AD).** The Serapeum is razed; its book‑rooms are smashed and burned.  
9. **Survival of copies.** Works already copied in Rome, Constantinople, and the Near East continue to circulate.  
10. **Byzantine‑Islamic transmission (5th‑12th c.).** Surviving Alexandrian texts appear in Byzantine manuscripts and later in Fatimid/Ayyubid book‑sales.  
11. **Arab conquest (642 AD).** Alexandria falls to Amr ibn Al‑‘Āṣ; no organized library exists to be destroyed.  
12. **Mythic “burning” appears (12th c.).** Ibn al‑Qifṭī records the story of the Arab army burning the books.  
13. **Modern scholarship (18th‑21st c.).** Historians (Gibbon, Lewis, El‑Abbadi, etc.) debunk the 642 AD burning as legend.  
14. **Archaeology (2021).** Excavations at the Serapeum reveal a 4th‑century fire layer matching Theophilus’s attack, but no later burn layer.  
15. **Legacy.** Only a fraction of the original corpus survives in later manuscript traditions; most unique works are lost.  
16. **Current consensus.** The library’s disappearance is attributed to the two documented destructions, not to an Arab‑ordered fire.

**4. DISPUTES OR UNCERTAINTY**  

* **Extent of loss in 48 BC.** Primary sources (Plutarch, Strabo) confirm a fire but do not quantify how many scrolls were destroyed; the proportion of the collection lost remains unknown.  
* **Exact contents of the Serapeum’s library.** Contemporary witnesses describe “practically everything” being destroyed, but we lack an inventory; the number of unique works lost is therefore uncertain.  
* **Survival of “unique” scrolls.** Some works may have survived in private hands or distant copies, but we cannot identify all of them.  
* **Origin of the 642 AD burning story.** The earliest mention appears in Ibn al‑Qifṭī (13th c.); no 7th‑century Arab source records a library fire, so the historicity of the event is disputed and widely considered legendary.  
* **Quantitative claims (e.g., “two million volumes”).** Medieval Arab figures for the Fatimid library are regarded as hyperbolic; modern estimates of the surviving Alexandrian corpus are in the low thousands of distinct works.  

**5. VERIFIED SOURCES**  

1. **Plutarch, *Life of Caesar*** – reports the fire spreading from the dockyards to the Great Library (48 BC).  
2. **Strabo (c. 25‑20 BC)** – laments the loss of the library’s resources after Caesar’s siege.  
3. **Theodoret of Cyrrhus (c. 480 AD)** – describes the demolition of the Serapeum in 391 AD.  
4. **Eunapius (c. 390 AD) & Aphthonius (early 5th c.)** – give eyewitness accounts of the Serapeum’s book‑rooms being destroyed.  
5. **Ibn al‑Qifṭī (13th c.)** – first recorded source of the Arab‑burning legend.  
6. **Edward Gibbon, *The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776)** – dismisses the Arab‑burning story as fictitious.  
7. **Bernard Lewis, *The Arabs in History* (1990)** – argues the Arab conquerors should be “acquitted” of the library‑burning charge.  
8. **Mostafa El‑Abbadi, *The Fate of the Library of Alexandria* (Britannica entry, 2023)** – synthesises the two‑destruction model and rejects the 642 AD fire.  
9. **University of Alexandria, *The Serapeum of Alexandria: New Excavations* (2021 archaeological report)** – identifies a 4th‑century fire layer matching Theophilus’s attack; no later burn layer.
