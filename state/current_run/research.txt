**1. CORE ANSWER**  
The Titanic’s wireless officers did receive several ice‑warning telegrams on the night of 14 April 1912, but the messages were not deliberately ignored. They were delayed or never reached the bridge because of a combination of (1) Marconi’s commercial policy that gave priority to passenger telegrams unless a message bore the special “MSG” (Master Service Gram) prefix, (2) recent technical problems that left the ship’s spark‑gap set barely functional and forced the crew to spend hours clearing a backlog of passenger traffic, (3) the wireless‑protocol conventions of the era (only “MSG” messages had to be passed to the bridge), (4) human factors – the operators had been working long, exhausting shifts and were handling multiple stations at once, and (5) the lack of a formal duty to forward every ice report. The official U.S. Senate and British Wreck Commissioner's inquiries concluded that the operators acted in accordance with the instructions they had received; the failure was systemic, not a personal act of negligence.

**2. STORY BEATS**  

1. **12 Apr – Equipment failure:** Titanic’s Marconi set breaks down; Phillips and Bride spend ~6 h repairing a transformer.  
2. **13 Apr – Backlog begins:** While the set is down, hundreds of passenger telegrams accumulate.  
3. **13 Apr ≈ 12 h – First ice warning:** Baltic (White Star) sends an ice‑berg warning; Phillips acknowledges and forwards it to the bridge (recorded in the Senate inquiry).  
4. **14 Apr 17:35 – Californian’s first call:** “Three large bergs 5 mi southward of us.” Phillips replies “DDD” (standard code meaning “stop transmitting”). Evans (Californian) interprets it as a routine end‑of‑transmission, not an insult.  
5. **14 Apr 21:30 – Mesaba warning:** “Drifting ice, large number of icebergs, ice field” arrives without the “MSG” prefix. Phillips logs it but continues sending passenger traffic to Cape Race; the bridge likely never sees it.  
6. **14 Apr 22:55 – Californian’s second call:** “We are stopped and surrounded by ice.” Same “DDD” reply; no further action.  
7. **Technical interference:** Spark‑gap transmitters cause overlapping signals; Californian’s strong transmission drowns out Cape Race’s weaker messages, adding to the confusion.  
8. **Operator fatigue:** By the time the critical messages arrive, Phillips has been on duty since ~19:30, exhausted, and under pressure to clear the passenger backlog.  
9. **Protocol gap:** Marconi policy requires only “MSG”‑marked telegrams to be taken to the bridge; most ice reports lack that tag, so operators are not obligated to interrupt passenger traffic.  
10. **Bridge response:** The bridge, having received the Baltic warning, relies on lookouts (“sharp lookout for ice”) but does not receive the later Mesaba or Californian warnings in time to alter speed or course.  
11. **Collision (23:40):** Titanic strikes iceberg; wireless operators continue sending distress CQDs and SOS.  
12. **Post‑disaster inquiries:** U.S. Senate and British Wreck Commissioner's inquiries attribute the failure to systemic procedural and commercial factors, not to willful neglect.  
13. **Regulatory change:** The disaster leads to the 1914 Radio Act and the 1915 SOLAS convention, mandating 24‑hour radio watches and giving ice warnings priority over passenger traffic.

| **Jack Phillips** | Chief wireless operator on Titanic; handled incoming ice warnings and passenger traffic. |
| **Harold Bride** | Assistant wireless operator; later survivor; testified at inquiries. |

**4. DISPUTES OR UNCERTAINTY**  

| Issue | Points of Contention | Current Evidence |
|-------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Did Phillips deliberately ignore ice warnings?** | Some popular accounts claim willful neglect to keep speed high. | Inquiries show Phillips followed Marconi protocol; no evidence of intentional dismissal. |
| **Was the “DDD” reply a rude command (“shut up”) or a routine code?** | Myth: Phillips insulted Californian. | Evans testified he understood “DDD” as the normal request to stop transmitting, not as an insult. |
| **Did the bridge actually receive the Mesaba warning?** | Conflicting statements about whether the warning was logged on bridge. | Senate transcript shows Phillips logged the message but continued passenger traffic; no record of bridge receipt. |
| **How much did commercial pressure influence operator priorities?** | Some argue commercial traffic should never outrank safety. | Marconi policy documents (cited in Hughes & Bosworth 2012) state only “MSG” messages required immediate forwarding; passenger traffic was given priority otherwise. |
| **Extent of technical failure impact** | Whether the earlier equipment breakdown significantly reduced capacity. | Testimony confirms a six‑hour repair on 13 Apr and ongoing instability; operators were still clearing backlog when ice warnings arrived. |
