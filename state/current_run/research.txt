  "CORE ANSWER": "Walking through a doorway creates an *event boundary* that forces the brain to update its mental representation of the current situation. This update automatically suppresses information that was relevant only to the just‑ended event – including the intention you were carrying (e.g., ‘I need coffee’). The memory isn’t erased; it becomes temporarily less active until a cue in the new environment (the coffee mug, the smell of coffee, etc.) re‑activates it. This phenomenon is known as the “doorway effect” and is a specific case of a broader event‑boundary‑driven forgetting in prospective memory.",
  "STORY BEATS": [
    "1. You form an intention in one location (e.g., at your desk you decide to get coffee).",
    "2. You move toward a doorway while holding that intention in working memory.",
    "3. Crossing the doorway registers as an event boundary in the hippocampal‑medial‑temporal system.",
    "4. The brain creates a new context tag for the new room and tags the old one as inactive.",
    "5. Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex briefly resets, freeing resources for the new episode.",
    "6. The cue‑intention link (room → coffee) is down‑regulated, making the intention less accessible.",
    "7. You experience a momentary feeling of “I forgot why I’m here.”",
    "8. A salient cue in the new room (a coffee mug, kettle, smell) triggers reinstatement of the intention.",
    "9. The intention resurfaces, often before you consciously notice the lapse.",
    "10. If no cue appears, the intention may remain dormant until you later retrieve it or it decays."
  ],
  "MECHANISM / CAUSE AND EFFECT": "Event‑boundary processing (hippocampus creates a new context tag; medial temporal lobe encodes spatial layout; dorsolateral PFC holds the prospective intention). Crossing a doorway signals a boundary, causing a brief ‘reset’ that suppresses the old cue‑intention association. The suppression is the cause of the temporary forgetting; the presence of a relevant cue in the new environment is the effect that restores the memory.",

  "MISCONCEPTIONS OR CONTRADICTIONS": [
    "“It’s just distraction.” – Forgetting occurs even when attention is explicitly directed to the intention (Radvansky & Copeland 2006).",
    "“Context‑dependent memory only.” – The effect persists when participants are returned to the original room, showing it’s not merely a mismatch of context (Scientific American 2011 summary).",
    "“Only doors matter.” – Any salient event boundary (sound, task change) produces a similar dip (Swallow et al. 2009)."
