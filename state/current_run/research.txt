**1. CORE ANSWER**  
The “massive wall” mentioned in the *Iliad* is **not** a permanent stone fortification built by the Greeks around the city of Troy. It is a **temporary defensive wall (with ditch) erected by the Achaean army around their fleet and camp** after they landed on the Trojan plain. Its purpose was to protect the ships, the booty taken from the city, and the Greek encampment during a protracted siege.  

The **real massive stone walls that surround the citadel of Troy** (the “Cyclopean” limestone walls of Troy VI‑VIIa, c. 1700‑1200 BC) were **constructed by the Trojans themselves**, as shown by the excavations of Schliemann, Dorpfeld, Blegen and later scholars. Homer’s description of a “lofty, angled wall” matches these archaeological remains, but the Greeks never added a permanent stone wall to the city.  

In the epic, the Achaean wall’s construction, its divine sanction (or lack thereof), and its eventual destruction by the gods become a pivotal narrative device: the wall secures the Greeks for years, its collapse forces a Trojan assault, triggers the death of Patroclus, the return of Achilles, and ultimately the final fall of Troy.  

---

**2. STORY BEATS** *(10‑16 numbered beats for a screenplay or documentary segment)*  

10. **Landing and Camp‑Setup** – The Achaean fleet arrives on the Trojan plain; the Greeks unload men, weapons and loot.  
11. **Building the Wall** – Under Agamemnon’s orders the Greeks dig a surrounding ditch and raise a high wooden‑earth wall to enclose their ships and camp (Iliad 12.1‑23).  
12. **Divine Ambivalence** – Homer notes the wall was built “without the consent of the immortals,” hinting that its durability depends on divine favor.  
13. **Stalemate Secured** – The fortified perimeter lets the Greeks hold a defensive line for years, turning the conflict into a ten‑year siege rather than a quick raid.  
14. **Trojan Counter‑Attack** – Poseidon, Apollo and Zeus send storms and rain against the wall; the structure collapses (Iliad 12.30‑38).  
15. **Turning Point** – With the wall down, Trojans breach the Greek camp, leading to Patroclus’s death, Achilles’s re‑entry, and the shift in war momentum.  
16. **Final Assault on Troy** – The Greeks, now without a protective wall, focus on breaching Troy’s own massive stone citadel walls (the “Cyclopean” fortifications of Troy VI‑VIIa), culminating in the city’s fall.  

---

**3. IMPORTANT PEOPLE / PLACES / OBJECTS**  

- **Achilles, Agamemnon, Patroclus** – Greek heroes whose actions are directly affected by the wall’s presence or loss.  
- **Poseidon, Apollo, Zeus** – Gods whose intervention causes the wall’s destruction.  
- **Troy VI‑VIIa citadel** – The real Bronze‑Age hill‑top city with massive limestone walls built by the Trojans.  
- **Achaean (Greek) wall** – Temporary wooden/earth fortification around the Greek fleet (Iliad 12).  
- **Scaean Gate / “angle of the lofty wall”** – Specific part of Troy’s stone wall described by Homer and matched archaeologically.  

---

**4. DISPUTES OR UNCERTAINTY**  

- **Identification of the “massive wall”** – Popular media sometimes conflate the Achaean field‑wall with Troy’s stone citadel walls; scholarly consensus separates the two.  
- **Exact construction details of the Achaean wall** – The *Iliad* gives a poetic description; no archaeological trace exists, so its precise materials (wood, earth, palisade) remain inferred from Near‑Eastern siege‑camp parallels.  
- **Historical vs. mythic chronology** – The ten‑year war, the divine motives, and the precise timeline are literary constructs; the archaeological record shows a destruction layer at Troy VIIa but cannot confirm the exact narrative sequence.  
- **Degree of Homeric memory** – While the description of Troy’s “lofty, angled wall” aligns with the limestone fortifications, it is uncertain how directly Homer’s poet accessed this knowledge (oral tradition, local lore, later poetic embellishment).
